# The Spiral Curriculum: An Exhaustive Analysis of Japanese Public School English Textbook Syllabi, Target Language, and Vocabulary Acquisition (Grades 3-9)

## 1. Introduction: The Paradigm Shift in Japanese English Education

The landscape of English Language Teaching (ELT) in Japanese public schools has undergone a seismic shift following the full implementation of the new Course of Study by the Ministry of Education, Culture, Sports, Science and Technology (MEXT) in 2020 for elementary schools and 2021 for junior high schools. This reform was not merely an adjustment of content but a fundamental restructuring of the linguistic milestones expected of Japanese learners. The transition effectively lowered the starting age of formal English engagement from Grade 5 to Grade 3 and shifted the status of English in Grades 5 and 6 from "Foreign Language Activities" to a formally assessed "Subject."

The implications of this shift are profound. Students entering Junior High School (JHS) are now expected to have a foundational vocabulary of 600–700 words and a working knowledge of basic sentence structures, such as the *can* auxiliary for ability and the past tense of high-frequency verbs. This has necessitated a complete rewriting of JHS textbooks, which must now bridge the gap between the audio-lingual, communication-focused elementary curriculum and the literacy-heavy, grammatical rigor of secondary education.

This report provides a comprehensive, granular analysis of the textbook syllabi currently in use across Japan. It dissects the unit progressions, target language (key sentences), and vocabulary clusters of the dominant publishers—Tokyo Shoseki (*New Horizon*), Mitsumura Tosho (*Here We Go!*), Kairyudo (*Sunshine*), and Sanseido (*New Crown*). Furthermore, it investigates the "Foreign Language Activities" curriculum (*Let's Try!*) used in Grades 3 and 4, analyzing how these early years lay the phonological and affective groundwork for the academic rigors that follow.2

The analysis is grounded in the concept of the "Spiral Curriculum," where specific linguistic functions (e.g., introducing oneself, describing daily routines, expressing desires) are revisited repeatedly across grades, with each iteration adding grammatical complexity and lexical depth. By examining these textbooks unit by unit, we reveal the specific pedagogical mechanisms MEXT employs to guide students from simple phatic communion to complex narrative production.

## 2. Foundations of Communication: Grades 3 and 4 (Foreign Language Activities)

In Grades 3 and 4, English is categorized as "Foreign Language Activities" (*Gaikokugo Katsudo*). The primary objective is not grammatical accuracy or literacy, but "familiarization" with the sounds and rhythms of English and the fostering of a positive attitude toward communication. The curriculum is standardized largely through the *Let's Try!* series, developed by MEXT in collaboration with major publishers.

### 2.1 The Grade 3 Curriculum: Let's Try! 1

The *Let's Try! 1* syllabus represents the Japanese student's first formal encounter with the English language in the public school system. The curriculum is divided into nine units, each designed to span approximately four to five lesson hours, totaling 35 hours per year. The pedagogical focus is heavily weighted toward listening and speaking, with a specific emphasis on noticing the differences between Japanese and English sounds.5

#### 2.1.1 Unit Analysis and Target Language

The progression of units in *Let's Try! 1* moves from the immediate self (feelings, likes) to the external world (counting, giving gifts, identifying objects).

| Unit | Title | Communicative Goal | Target Language (Key Sentences) | Vocabulary & Cultural Focus |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Hello! | Greetings & Self-Introduction | "Hello." "I'm [Name]." "Nice to meet you." | Vocab: Hello, Hi, Good morning/afternoon/evening, Goodbye, See you.  Culture: World greetings (Bonjour, Nihao, Jambo). |
| 2 | How are you? | Expressing Feelings | "How are you?" "I'm [happy/good/sleepy]." | Vocab: fine, happy, good, sleepy, hungry, tired, sad, great.  Activity: Gesture games to link physical affect with adjectives. |
| 3 | How many? | Counting & Plurals | "How many [apples]?" "One, two, three..." | Vocab: Numbers 1–20. Classroom objects (ruler, eraser).  Grammar: Implicit introduction of plural 's' (listening only). |
| 4 | I like blue. | Expressing Likes (Colors) | "I like [blue]." "Do you like [blue]?" "Yes, I do. / No, I don't." | Vocab: red, blue, green, yellow, pink, orange, purple, black, white, rainbow.  Culture: Colors of the world's flags. |
| 5 | What do you like? | Likes (Food & Sports) | "What do you like?" "I like [blue/soccer]." | Vocab: Fruits (peach, melon, banana), Sports (soccer, baseball).  Phonics: Rhythms of Katakana words vs. English pronunciation. |
| 6 | ALPHABET | Letter Recognition | "A, B, C..." "What's this?" | Vocab: The 26 letters (Uppercase focus).  Goal: Recognizing letter shapes, not writing them. |
| 7 | This is for you. | Giving & Receiving | "What's this?" "It's a [card]." "This is for you." "Thank you." | Vocab: Shapes (circle, triangle, star, heart).  Activity: Making greeting cards. |
| 8 | Who are you? | Identifying Animals | "Who are you?" "I am a [lion]." "Are you a [rabbit]?" | Vocab: Animals (lion, tiger, rabbit, snake, cow, horse).  Sound: Animal onomatopoeia (e.g., "Moo" vs. "Mo-mo"). |
| 9 | What's this? | Mystery Objects / Review | "What's this?" "Hint, please." "It's a [fruit]." | Consolidation: Review of numbers, colors, and animals. |

#### 2.1.2 Pedagogical Analysis of Grade 3

The *Let's Try! 1* syllabus exhibits several strategic pedagogical choices aimed at reducing cognitive load:

**Avoidance of the Copula:** The verb *be* is not taught as a grammatical concept. In Unit 1, "I'm [Name]" is treated as a single lexical chunk synonymous with "My name is." This prevents early confusion regarding contraction rules or subject-verb agreement.5

**Implicit Pluralization:** In Unit 3 ("How many?"), students are exposed to the plural 's' sound (e.g., "three apple**s**") purely through listening. They are not expected to produce this distinction consistently or understand the morphological rule. This aligns with the MEXT guideline of "Sound First," prioritizing the development of a "English ear" before enforcing grammatical accuracy.4

**Katakana Correction:** A significant portion of the vocabulary list (e.g., *banana, melon, orange, soccer, bus*) consists of words that exist as loanwords in Japanese (Katakana-go). The curriculum explicitly targets these words to correct pronunciation (e.g., fixing the vowel insertion in "o-ren-ji" to "orange").3

### 2.2 The Grade 4 Curriculum: Let's Try! 2

Grade 4 serves as a critical bridge. While still classified as "Foreign Language Activities," the *Let's Try! 2* syllabus introduces significantly more complex sentence structures, including the auxiliary *do* for questions and negatives, and begins the transition toward literacy with lowercase letters.

#### 2.2.1 Unit Analysis and Target Language

The syllabus expands the student's world from personal likes to daily routines and wider environments.6

| Unit | Title | Communicative Goal | Target Language (Key Sentences) | Vocabulary & Context |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Hello, world! | Global Greetings | "Hello. I'm [Name]." "I like [origami]." | Vocab: Countries (Japan, USA, Korea, Brazil, Australia).  Grammar: Review of "I like" + Noun. |
| 2 | Let's play cards. | Weather & Play | "How's the weather?" "It's [sunny]." "Let's play [tag]." | Vocab: Weather (sunny, rainy, cloudy, snowy). Games (tag, cards, hide-and-seek). |
| 3 | I like Mondays. | Days of the Week | "What day is it?" "It's [Monday]." "I like [lunch time]." | Vocab: Days (Mon-Sun). School subjects (math, PE). |
| 4 | What time is it? | Time & Routine | "What time is it?" "It's 1 o'clock." "It's [snack] time." | Vocab: Numbers 1–12. Routines (wake up, lunch, sleep).  Note: Uses "It's time for..." structure. |
| 5 | Do you have a pen? | Possession | "Do you have a [pen]?" "Yes, I do. / No, I don't." | Vocab: Stationery (pen, eraser, ruler, pencil case).  Grammar: First explicit use of Do auxiliary for questions. |
| 6 | Alphabet | Lowercase Letters | "a, b, c..." "Do you have a 'j'?" | Vocab: Lowercase letters.  Activity: Identifying initial sounds (Phonics foundation). |
| 7 | What do you want? | Wants & Requests | "What do you want?" "I want a [peach], please." "Here you are." | Vocab: Ingredients (pizza, parfait). Fruits (plurals).  Grammar: Introduction of want. |
| 8 | This is my favorite place. | Directions | "Go straight." "Turn right/left." "This is the [library]." | Vocab: School rooms (library, gym, nurse's office). Directions.  Activity: Guiding a blindfolded partner. |
| 9 | This is my day. | Daily Routine (Verbs) | "I [get up] at." "I [go to school]." | Vocab: Action Verbs (wash face, brush teeth, put on clothes). |

#### 2.2.2 Pedagogical Pivot: The Introduction of Verbs

Unit 9 ("This is my day") represents a massive leap in linguistic demand. For the first time, students move away from the static "I like [Noun]" or "I have [Noun]" structures to dynamic Subject-Verb structures ("I get up," "I go"). This unit is strategically placed at the end of Grade 4 to scaffold the entry into Grade 5, where verb conjugation and usage become central. The vocabulary here focuses on high-frequency daily verbs that allow students to narrate their own lives, fostering a sense of ownership over the language.6

Additionally, Unit 6 shifts focus to **lowercase letters**. MEXT guidelines emphasize that lowercase recognition is more critical for reading actual text than uppercase, which dominated Grade 3. This unit often employs "Sounds and Letters" modules—short, rhythmic chants linking the letter shape to its phonemic sound (e.g., "b, b, bed"), effectively acting as a stealth phonics program.9

## 3. English as a Subject: Grades 5 and 6 (The "Kyoka" Curriculum)

In Grades 5 and 6, English transitions to a formal subject (*Kyoka*). This status change brings graded assessment, government-approved textbooks, and a mandatory vocabulary target of 600–700 words. The curriculum is designed to spiral upward from the foundations laid in *Let's Try!*, moving from simple chunks to creating original sentences.

Three major publishers dominate this market: Tokyo Shoseki (*New Horizon Elementary*), Mitsumura Tosho (*Here We Go!*), and Kairyudo (*Junior Sunshine*). While they all follow MEXT's Course of Study, their approaches to syllabus organization differ significantly.11

### 3.1 Grade 5 Analysis: New Horizon Elementary 5 vs. Here We Go! 5

Grade 5 is where the "Subject" designation is felt most acutely. Students must now begin writing (copying words and basic sentences) and are introduced to more abstract grammar concepts like "Can" for possibility and "Where" for location.

#### 3.1.1 Comparative Syllabus Table (Grade 5)

| Theme/Function | New Horizon 5 (Tokyo Shoseki) | Here We Go! 5 (Mitsumura Tosho) | Pedagogical Divergence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Self-Introduction | Unit 1: Hello, friends.  "I like [soccer]. I have [a dog]." | Unit 1: Hello, everyone.  "My name is... I like..." | New Horizon introduces have immediately. Here We Go! stays on like. |
| Birthdays/Dates | Unit 2: When is your birthday?  "It's [June 12th]." | Unit 2: When is your birthday?  "What do you want for your birthday?" | Both focus on dates, but Here We Go! integrates "wants" earlier. |
| Daily Routine | Unit 4: What time do you get up?  "I [get up] at." | Unit 4: What time do you get up?  "I usually take out the garbage." | Here We Go! explicitly teaches frequency adverbs (always, usually), increasing complexity. |
| Locations | Unit 5: Where is the post office?  Directions (Turn right). | (Covered in Grade 6) | New Horizon prioritizes spatial directions in Grade 5. |
| Ordering Food | Unit 6: What would you like?  "I'd like [pizza]." | Unit 7: What would you like?  "How much is it?" | Both introduce polite requests (would). |
| Ability (Can) | Unit 7: Welcome to Japan.  "You can [see Mt. Fuji]." | Unit 5: He can run fast.  "He/She can..." | New Horizon uses Can for tourism/possibility (CLIL). Here We Go! uses Can for personal ability. |
| Desires/Travel | (Covered in Grade 6) | Unit 6: I want to go to Italy.  "I want to go to..." | Here We Go! introduces the "want to" infinitive structure a full year earlier. |

#### 3.1.2 Deep Dive: Target Language and Vocabulary

**The "Can" Shift:** In *New Horizon* Unit 7, the usage of *can* shifts from the personal "I can swim" (ability) to the situational "You can see Mt. Fuji" (possibility). This subtle shift is highly sophisticated, introducing the concept that modal verbs change meaning based on context. It also links to Social Studies (Geography of Japan), a hallmark of the CLIL approach.13

**Third Person Pronouns:** *New Horizon* Unit 8 ("Who is your hero?") introduces *He* and *She*. Crucially, the syllabus avoids the third-person singular 's' (e.g., "He is good at soccer" rather than "He plays soccer"). This deliberate scaffolding delays the morphological difficulty of subject-verb agreement until Grade 6 or JHS, allowing students to use the pronouns without grammatical penalty.14

### 3.2 Grade 6 Analysis: The Past Tense and Future Dreams

Grade 6 is defined by two major linguistic hurdles: the **Past Tense** and the **Infinitive** (expressing purpose/desire). These are the pillars of narrative construction, moving students from "describing the now" to "recounting the past" and "projecting the future."

#### 3.2.1 Comparative Syllabus Table (Grade 6)

| Theme/Function | New Horizon 6 | Here We Go! 6 | Key Differences |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Self & Culture | Unit 1: This is me.  "My treasure is..." | Unit 1: This is me.  "My name is..." | New Horizon focuses on identifying "Treasures" (values). |
| Tourism | Unit 2: Welcome to Japan.  Performance Task: Guide. | Unit 2: Welcome to Japan.  Describing seasons/events. | New Horizon emphasizes performance (acting as a guide). |
| International | Unit 3: Let's go to Italy.  "I want to go to..." | (Covered in Grade 5) | New Horizon introduces the "want to" structure here. |
| Past Tense | Unit 4: Summer Vacations.  "I went to..." | Unit 3: Summer Vacation.  "I enjoyed..." | Both use the "Summer Vacation" recount genre to introduce past verbs. |
| Biography | Unit 5: He is famous.  "He is a [musician]." | Unit 5: He can run fast.  Ability focus. | New Horizon uses biographies to practice 3rd person extensively. |
| Future/Dreams | Unit 6: Junior High Life.  "I want to join..." | Unit 7: My Future.  "I want to be..." | Both link grammar (want to) with career education (Career Education). |
| Memories | Unit 7: My Best Memory.  Past Tense Narrative. | Unit 8: Graduation.  Recalling school events. | Consolidation of past tense irregular verbs. |

#### 3.2.2 The Past Tense Crisis and Resolution

The introduction of the past tense in Grade 6 (usually Unit 4 in *New Horizon* and Unit 3 in *Here We Go!*) is traditionally a friction point. The syllabus manages this by restricting the vocabulary to high-frequency irregular verbs (*went, ate, saw, did*) and regular verbs (*enjoyed, played*).

**Target Language:** "I went to [place]." "I enjoyed [activity]." "It was [fun]."

**Assessment Focus:** The goal is communication of the *experience*, not perfect conjugation. MEXT guidelines suggest that errors like "I goed" should be corrected gently or implicitly, rather than penalized, to maintain communicative confidence.4

**Narrative Structure:** Students are taught a specific discourse structure for these units:

**Topic:** "I went to the beach."

**Detail:** "I swam in the sea."

Feeling: "It was exciting."
This 3-step structure is the foundation of the "Performance Tests" often administered at the end of the term.

## 4. The Junior High School Transition: 2021 Curriculum Overhaul

The 2021 revision of JHS textbooks was a direct response to the changes in Elementary School. Previously, JHS Year 1 (Grade 7) started from the alphabet and "This is a pen." Now, it assumes students can read, write basic words, and understand simple sentence structures. The result is a **compressed and accelerated syllabus**.11

### 4.1 The "Gap" Semester: Unit 0 and the New Starting Line

All major JHS textbooks (*New Horizon, Sunshine, New Crown*) now begin with a "Unit 0" or "Pre-Lesson" module. This module is a rapid-fire review of Elementary School English, covering *can*, *like*, *want to*, and basic *past tense* in the first 2-3 weeks of April.

**Implication:** This means the "slow entry" period of JHS is gone. Students must hit the ground running with functional communication skills.

### 4.2 Grade 7 (JHS 1) Syllabus Deep Dive: New Horizon vs. New Crown vs. Sunshine

The Grade 7 syllabus now includes grammar points previously taught in Grade 8, such as the *be* + *ing* (Present Continuous) and a more robust treatment of the Past Tense.

| Unit | Grammar Point | New Horizon 1 (Tokyo Shoseki) | New Crown 1 (Sanseido) | Sunshine 1 (Kairyudo) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Early Units | Be-verb / General Verbs | Unit 1: "I am Ellen Baker." "Are you...?"  Unit 2: "This is..." / "He is..."  Unit 3: "I like math." (Do/Don't) | Lesson 1: "I am..."  Lesson 2: "Do you...?"  Focus: Heavier emphasis on "Thinking" and "Logic" early on. | Program 1: "I am..."  Program 2: "This is..."  Feature: "Power-Up" sections integrate CLIL (e.g., Math shadows) early. |
| Mid Units | 3rd Person / Pronouns | Unit 5: "Haruki lives in Australia."  Unit 6: Pronouns (him, his, mine). | Lesson 4: 3rd Person Singular.  Project: "Introduce Your Town." | Program 5: 3rd Person.  Feature: Explicit "Retelling" tasks marked for speaking tests. |
| Late Units | Continuous / Modals / Past | Unit 7: Present Continuous (-ing).  Unit 9: Past Tense (Regular).  Unit 10: Past Tense (Irregular). | Lesson 7: Past Tense.  Lesson 8: "Memories" (Past narrative). | Program 8: Past Tense.  Program 9: "Looking Back" (narrative integration). |

**Structural Analysis:**

**The "Spiral" Re-visited:** Note that *Can*, *Like*, *Want*, and *Past Tense* appeared in Elementary School. In JHS Unit 0–1, they are reviewed functionally. However, in Unit 5 (3rd Person Singular) and Unit 9 (Past Tense), they are **formalized** with strict grammatical rules (e.g., *Do* becoming *Does*, *Did* usage in questions). The curriculum relies on the student's "ear" developed in ES to make these rules adhere.

**Vocabulary Explosion:** The target vocabulary for JHS has increased to 1,600–1,800 words. This includes a massive influx of abstract words related to the SDGs (Sustainable Development Goals), which MEXT has mandated across the curriculum. Words like *sustainable, environment, plastic, equality,* and *energy* now appear in Grade 7 and 8 textbooks, significantly raising the reading level required.16

## 5. Comprehensive Vocabulary Analysis: From "Friendly" to "Academic"

The most drastic change in the 2020/2021 reforms is the volume and nature of the vocabulary. The system now operates on a two-tier vocabulary model.

### 5.1 The "Friendly 700" (Elementary School)

The 600–700 words targeted in ES are chosen based on immediate relevance to a child's life and high-frequency loanwords.

**Cluster 1: School Life:** *pencil, eraser, gym, library, principal, subject, recess, homework.*

**Cluster 2: Food & Dining:** *curry and rice, spaghetti, parfait, bitter, sweet, salty, delicious.*

*Note:* "Parfait" and "curry and rice" are specifically included because they are "Anglicized" Japanese cultural staples, and learning their English pronunciation helps break Katakana habits.

**Cluster 3: Global Culture:** *Halloween, Easter, Christmas, New Year's Day.*

**Cluster 4: Emotional Adjectives:** *cool, cute, scary, beautiful, nice, great.*

*Pedagogical Insight:* "Cute" (*kawaii*) and "Cool" (*kakkoii*) are high-frequency drivers for Japanese students' motivation and are used extensively in "Small Talk" modules to elicit reactions.

### 5.2 The "Academic 1800" (Junior High School)

JHS textbooks introduce abstract and low-frequency words much earlier than in the past.

**Early Grade 7:** *amusement park, department store, local, traditional.*

**SDGs Vocabulary:** *environment, plastic, waste, solar, poverty, gender.*

**Discourse Markers:** *First, Next, Then, Finally* (for storytelling); *However, Therefore, For example* (for logic/debate).

*Impact:* The inclusion of discourse markers in Grade 7/8 signals a shift from "sentence-level" English to "paragraph-level" English. Students are expected to structure speeches, not just answer questions.

### 5.3 Word Lists and the NGSL

Research comparing JHS textbooks to the **New General Service List (NGSL)** indicates that while the coverage of high-frequency words is good, the "Publisher-Specific" vocabulary (words chosen by Sanseido, Tokyo Shoseki, etc., outside the MEXT mandatory list) often varies wildly. This creates a situation where a student transferring from a school using *New Horizon* to one using *New Crown* might face a vocabulary gap of 200–300 words, particularly in thematic nouns (e.g., specific animals, hobbies, or cultural terms).16

## 6. Pedagogical Features and Assessment Strategies

The syllabi are not just lists of words; they are delivery systems for specific pedagogical methodologies mandated by MEXT.

### 6.1 Small Talk and "Aizuchi"

In *New Horizon Elementary*, every unit begins with a "Small Talk" module. This is an unscripted (or semi-scripted) chat between the Homeroom Teacher (HRT) and the Assistant Language Teacher (ALT).

**Target:** Modeling natural reaction strategies (*Aizuchi*).

**Key Expressions:** "Really?", "Wow!", "Me too!", "I see.", "That's nice."

**Significance:** This elevates "Reacting" to a core skill, equal to Speaking or Listening. The goal is to train students to be active listeners rather than passive receivers.19

### 6.2 CLIL (Content and Language Integrated Learning)

Textbooks increasingly integrate content from other subjects to provide context for English use.

**Math:** *New Horizon ES Grade 5* Unit 6 ("How much is it?") involves calculation and currency.

**Social Studies:** *Here We Go! Grade 6* Unit 3 ("Let's go to Italy") involves geography and World Heritage Sites.

**Moral Education:** *New Crown JHS* frequently features units on historical figures (e.g., Malala Yousafzai, Rosa Parks) to teach justice and courage alongside grammar, aligning with the "Moral Education" subject in the Japanese curriculum.21

### 6.3 Performance Assessment

The shift to "Subject" status in ES means students must be graded. The syllabi are designed to support **Performance Tests**.

**Retelling Tasks:** *Sunshine* JHS textbooks explicitly mark sections for "Retelling," where students read a text and then must summarize it to a partner.

**Presentation Tasks:** ES Unit 8s are almost universally designed as "Project" units (e.g., "My Future Dream" speech), providing a clear artifact for teacher assessment.

### 6.4 Digital Textbooks

As of 2024, the introduction of digital textbooks has added a new layer to the syllabus. These digital versions include embedded audio for every word, "karaoke" style reading highlighting, and interactive "reaction" buttons. This supports the "Sounds and Letters" modules by allowing students to hear correct phonics models independently of the teacher's ability.23

## 7. Conclusion: The Spiral Continues

The analysis of the Japanese public school English textbook landscape reveals a highly structured, interconnected system that has matured significantly since the 2020 reforms. The Elementary curriculum is no longer merely "playtime" or cultural exposure; it is a carefully scaffolded introduction to the lexical chunks and phonological awareness required for the academic rigor of Junior High School.

The "Target Language" has evolved from rote memorization of grammar rules to functional "Key Sentences" that serve specific communicative purposes (inviting, offering, requesting, reacting). However, the burden on students has increased substantially. The total required vocabulary has effectively doubled from the pre-2020 era, and the pacing of JHS Grade 1 is unforgiving for students who do not master the Elementary content.

For educators, curriculum designers, and policy analysts, the critical insight is the **spiral nature** of the syllabus. Materials designed for Grade 6 are not endpoints but launchpads for Grade 7. The "Gap" between primary and secondary education is being bridged not by slowing down JHS, but by accelerating ES. The challenge for the next decade of Japanese English education will be ensuring that this acceleration does not leave slower learners behind, particularly given the heavy reliance on "implied" grammar learning in the early years.

The integration of digital tools, the focus on SDGs, and the adoption of CLIL methodologies suggest that Japanese textbooks are aligning more closely with global ELT trends, even as they retain distinctively Japanese pedagogical features like *Small Talk* and moral education integration.
